Biografi

Ruth Adelia Iverson ble født den 5 mai 1918 på/i Buchanan, Saskatchewan, Canada. Hun giftet seg med Robert Wilhelm Frivold den 29 juni 1946 på/i USA.
Memories of Father:
"I was one year and a half years old when my family came to
America. My father had nephews in Tacoma and Seattle, and it
was they who encouraged him to come to Washington State.
Another reason behind the decision to move to America was my
mother's health. After having had a stillbirth between Hegge
and me, Mother was in a weakened state. This condition was
further aggravated by an open abcess in her side that would not
heal. My father had heard of a medical specialist in Tacoma by
the name of Dr. Quivley who was considered to be an excellent
physician. For these reasons, the prospects of superior medical
treatment played an important role in our leaving Saskatchewan.
My earliest recollection of Yelm was our pretty yellow house,
trimmed in white around the windows, and a red roof. The barn,
woodshed, chicken house, and out-door privy were all painted
red with white trim. Years later, I saw this color scheeme
brighten the scenic countryside of northern Trøndelag. Now I
understand why Dad painted our buildings in these colors - an
attempt to recreate that which he remembered from his homeland,
Norway. Mother loved flowers and gardening and worked hard to
make an attractive and well-kept yard.
Dad acquired twenty-five acres of property on which he grew a
great many kinds of berries; strawberries, blackcaps,
blackberries, loganberries, and youngberries. My early
childhood was spent picking through August, ending the season
with loganberries and blackberries. One of the big highlights
after berry season was over, was a trip to Tacoma in order to
buy supplies and clothes for the new school year. A special
treat was to have a lovely dinner at Manning's Cafeteria of
roast beef, and for dessert there was pretty red jello with
whipping cream!
I vividly remember another happy event when I was a little
girl. One day I was upstairs, and as I looked out the window, I
saw some little windows in a big box out in the yard. I began
to wonder if these could be for a little playhouse? After much
speculation, my curiosity was durther aroused when my father
and a neighbor named Anton Johnson made a little floor under
our three big fir trees. Not being able to contain my
excitement any longer, I asked: "Daddy, what are you making?"
his curious reply was, "a little pig pen for a little piggy".
Confused I wondered why a pig pen was going to have a wooden
floor - as far as I knew about the nature of pigs they had to
have dirt, preferably mud, to wallow in - no matter how hard I
tried, I could not make the connection of pigs in a little
wooden house!
As the days went by, this little construction grew to be a
recognizable house, complete with six windows - two on each
side and one on either end under the peak of the roof. It was
painted yellow with the windows trimmed in white and a red roof
just like our big house. Some days later, I was presented with
every little girl's dream - a playhouse! Many years of happy
were enjoyed there with my many friends. Mother made little
curtains for the windows and scatter rugs for the floor. I
thought that I had the best Daddy in the world! As cars drove
slowly past our home and passerbys noticed this cute little
house, they would remark at what a lovely playhouse it was.
Being the rightsful resident og this structure, and responsible
for its upkeep, such complimentary comments made me very proud
indeed.
I remember when I used to ride out with my father in our
horse-drawn wagon to the woods in order to get wood for our
wood-burning stoves. Pur horse's name was "Tommy". One
particular day I did not go with him, and later that afternoon
when Daddy came walking home, I wondered where Tommy was and
why he wasn't pulling the wagon! As my father recounted the
story, Tommy was pulling the wagon as he always had, when
suddenly he fell dead in his tracks - probably because of a
heart attack. Later we got another horse and named him "Major",
but he was never quite the same as our Tommy!
Often, I would herd the cows to pasture about one and a half
miles from our home. In order to get to the pasture, I would
walk along the main highway, which runs from Yelm to Olympia -
this was often quite heavily trafficked. One evening as I was
on my way to bring the cows home for the night, a car drove
slowly by with two men in the front seat with what looked like
a lot of junk piled up on the back seat. As they approached me,
I overheard them say: !Let's stop and see what she will do." I
became so frightened that I jumped over a large fallen tree,
through bush and high grass, and didn't stop running, asking
Jesus to protect me, until I reached home. Needless to say, it
was a long time before I wanted to herd the cows again. My
parents were very concerned as well, and didn't want me to go
after them by myself.
My father always looked neat and would love to get dressed up.
Often after he had shaven and had put scented powder on his
face, he would come up to my mother and say: "Nei mamma, nu må
du kysse meg!" (Now Mother give me a kiss!).
Daddy always had white peppermints in the pockets of his good
suit, so I was always glad to see him put on his coat and go
someplace so I could have some delicious peppermints to suck
on. I would enjoy sitting in my father's lap and combing his
hair with a fine-tooth comb. I made all kinds of designs with
his sand.colored hair - though it was not exactly plentiful!
Another outstanding memory from my childhood was when an
airplane crashed into the back of our house. There was a large
open field that was adjacent to our property. One day an
airplane landed there. It was a small two-seater type plane.
From then on, the pilot would take passengers up for a short
ride every Sunday afternoon. Consequently, our yard became a
convinient parking lot for the passengers' cars when they would
go up for a ride. This meant that we had no privacy and being
Sunday, Mother hoped that these activities would discontinue.
However as small children, we all enjoyed the excitement of so
many people at our house with the yard full of facinating cars.
We felt very popular when articles about these activities would
appear in the Olympia and Tacoma papers. One particular Sunday
when my father came after us when church was over, he excitedly
began to recount the events of that morning when the airplane
had run into the back of our house. We came home to find that
this aircraft was at our back door! The pilot was evidently
taking a passenger up and as he ascended, the tail end of the
plane hit a fence post at the edge of the big field. This threw
the airplane out of control and instead og going up to the
right, as he intended, he veered to the left directly into the
back of our house. My father had been shaving, and just ready
to use the straight-edged razor, when the jold of the plane
shook the house, and knocked the razor from his hand. With
lather all over his face, he found the airplane with the
aviator and passenger starring raight at him! We really made
the headlines now - "Airplanes rams into Carl Iverson's home in
Yelm!" My father allowed the pilot to remove the motor from the
aircraft before an insurance settlement had been reached, and
so Dad was consequently left with the full cost of repairs. As
it turned out, a completely new room was built where the damage
had occured. For a number of years, we children played in the
plane's wreckage! Needless to say, this ill-fated flight ended
the Sunday spectacles at our home.
My father was quite politically active. He would often go to
Olympia to meet the Governor of the State, and other
politicians he supported. He was a staunch Democrat and worked
hard campaigning for candidates running for office. From a
local standpoint un Yelm, his lobbying efforts helped get
several men elected.
When I was about eight or nine years old, I once got into Dad's
car and laid down on the floor in the back, after hearing that
he was going to see Anton Johnson. He was the man who helped
Daddy build my playhouse, so I always knew he had something to
give me, and I liked his storebought bread, something which we
never had. So, when Daddy arrived at Mr. Johnson's house, I
gave him the fright of his life by jumping up from the back
seat and excitedly asked him what he was going to do at Mr.
Johnson's. Never suspecting a stoveaway in his back seat, he
became very angry with me because he was planning to go have a
glass of homebrew that Mr. Johnson had made. As Mother would
soon begin to worry about me, not knowing of my plans to invite
myself to the Johnson's, my father took me home imideately
without even having his glass of beer! I didn't get a spanking,
but was duly scolded by both my parents. Such was the ending of
an evening which I had anticipated to be very exciting.
Another unforgettable experience of growing up, was when I
accidentally threw my father's false teeth in the stove! I was
in high school and was going to a play rehersal so I was in a
hurry. As Mother was aout, I was left with washing the dishes
and cleaning up. I wanted Dad to quickly finish his dinner
because I wanted to tidy the kitchen before I left. At the
time, Dad worked at an elementary school as a custodian during
the school year and would take his lunch everyday in his lunch
box. I quickly cleared out his lunch box and felt something
hard in the wax paper. Thinking that it was a chicken bone, As
Mother often gave him a piece of chicken in his lunch, I threw
the wax paper and its contents into the stove and immediately
hear a sizzling sound. At that moment my father asked me for
his teeth as he had the habit of taking out his dentures when
the teachers left because they didn't fit him too well. I was
shocked when I realized what I had done. Dad's ensuing lecture
was: " One hundred dollar shot to hell! Now Ruthie, you must
help me clean the rooms at school without pay until you have
paid back this expence!" However, after he had regained his
composure, he waived that order.
The dentist made hin a new set for half the price. So, my
little accident turned out to be a blessing in disguise, as
Father got a new set of dentures which fit him better, for half
the price!
These are some of the many memories that I have of my father,
Carl Johan Hagerup Iverson. Growing up, I remember hearing the
names of "Bindal" and "Storheil" and would often wonder what
these places were like. Never did I imagine that one day, I
would have the opportunity to see first-hand the childhood home
of my father, but in April 1990 this unexpected wish was
fulfilled. Seeing the red house painted in white trim, it was
difficult to imagine how eight children could all have lived in
such a small dwelling. Nestled at the foot of a jagged granite
cliff, with the shores of Lysfjorden just a stone's throw away,
it was a solemn moment to be propelled back in time when Carl,
as a young boy, would run down to the water or climb old
"Bjørnson". It was here that Carl grew up to become the man I
knew as Father. Having a wealth of memories from his life in
America, I experienced the unique phenomenon of recapturing
what his life must have been like while in Norway. By
journeying to Storheil, I had completed the circle of Carl's
life and discovered that I added a new dimension to my own."

Memories of Mother:

" I am filled with gratitude and respect as I reflect on the
life of my wonderful mother. I learned so much of love,
compassion, honesty, and integrity of character from her. Her
spiritual guidance and counsel were of great significanse to
me. It is difficult to find the words to appropriately describe
the grat heritage which I feel that I have received. She was
gifted with the talent of making a home.
Mother would often help me make doll clothes for my dolls, as
well as weave rugs and curtains for my little playhouse. Her
appreciation for the beauty of nature was reflected in her
passion for flowers and gardening. She spent many afternoons
cultivating our yard into a lovely flower garden.
I remember two very special dresses which she made for me. One
was a tan ponge' which made me feel like a princess, the other
was a maroon velvet dress with french knots fashioned in two
circles on the vest and around the cuffs. One particular Sunday
morning, I was all dressed up ready for Sunday School. This was
the first time I was permitted to wear my special velvet
creation, and I proudly wore it, complimented with white socks
and lack patent teather slippers. The crown of my regal attire
was a magnificent white bow, set just so, in my blond hair.
I must admit, I thought I was quite attractive, and wanting to
just make sure, I decided to go and have a look at myself. Not
finding a mirror readily available, I skipped to the back door
where there ran an irrigation ditch. A plank had been put
across the ditch from which water could be drawn for washing
and scrubbing. Gingerly, I tiptoed onto the plank and knelt
down to behold my reflection below. Without ever getting a
proper look at myself, I plunged unceremoniously headfirst into
the murky water after losing my balance on the narrow plank.
Needless to say, I was promtly reprimanded and ordered to
change my clothes immediately. Feeling very disappointed at how
this all turned out, and perhaps feeling a little sorry for
myself, I cried for some time. Yet, after the tears subsided,
the lesson I learned was the importance of obedience and the
folly of vanity.
Mother was a wonderful cook and always made Sunday a special
day with a cloth tablecloth on our table. She often served
roast chicken with dressing, mashed potatoes with delicious
garden vegetables, and for dessert jello with bananas which was
a very special treat. Mother always baked, canned, and fixed
special Norwegian dishes when Dad's nephews came to visit.
Among her specialities were: "lefse", "berlingerkranse",
"fattigmand", "primost", and "gumme". She would often stay up
until the early hours of the morning preparing these for
company. Often my cousins would come unannounced and woul
invariably stay for several days.
One of my early childhood chores was to put clean paper in the
cooler every week, and to polish the silver when guests were
coming.
When as was eleven years old, I sprained my ankle while playing
baseball with some of our berry pickers. (My father had a
separate house which accomodated these workers for the summer.)
As a result of this injury, a year later, I contracted
tuberculosis of the bone in the ankle. In june 1931, at twelve
years of age, I was operated on by a bone specialist by the
name of Dr. Rich of Tacoma. Three ankle joints were susequently
removed and casts and crutches were part of my growing up
years. During this time, I would sit on a chair and peel
apples, potatoes, carrots, and other things for Mother. I
always wanted to help with something even though I could not
walk without the aid of crutches.
During these years of convalescing, Mother shared many of her
childhood and adulthood memories with me about Norway and her
desire to serve God with her life. One experience which she
would recall, had a profound influence on my life. She was home
in Bindalen. After her fiance' drowned, she thought she would
never marry, yet hoped that God would use her life. One day as
she was herding the cows out in the forested pastures of
Vikestad, she had a vision. She had been praying, asking God to
use her life, when in this vision she saw an open Bible toward
which a finger pointed. Around the Bible she saw children of
another race and culture, very foreign in appearance and not
European. Later, when Carl Iversen came into her life, she
contemplated; "perhaps if I marry Carl, I will find these
children in the new country to which he would take me."
Although years passed, having moved to Canada and subsequently
to Washington state, she never forgot this vision and began to
pray for her children. Being crippled, she would often pray for
my healing and would say: "Ryth, give God your life, maybe He
would have you to become a missionary". My reply was: "Mamma,
don't be silly - God needs for me to have two good feet if I am
to work for Him". However, she kept praying for me and my
future. Finally, after seven years of being crippled and a
second operation, the doctors thought that they would have to
amputate my foot. My condition had turned for the worse. But,
due to the prayers of a faithful and wonderful mother, I was
miraculously healed. The night that God healed me, I was alone
in my bedrom as 12:30 at night. It was then that I made my
uncompromissing commitment to Jesus Christ and, if He so chose,
to be a missionary to the part of the world to which the little
children of Mother's vision had belonged - Asia.
Mother's vision of years before would eventually be realized as
I arrived in Tokyo together with my husband and two daughters
in May of 1952. Thirty years of missionary work in Japan was
the fulfilled life which God gave, and I shall never cease to
thank God for His faithfulness as well as for the prayers of my
mother. Her life has inspired faith in her children and is a
priceless heritage for generations to come.
Journeying to the wooded farm of Vikestad was a dream come
true. To see where Mother's home once stood, the pastures where
she herded the cows, and the clear waters of Bindalsfjorden
across which she journeyed on her wedding day, all brought an
indescribable sense of wonder and joy! Beholding the land of my
forefathers gave me a new sense of identity and history.
Reflecting on the life of my mother also filled me with awe at
God's faithfulness from generation to generation. As we commit
our steps to the Lord, He shall bring it to pass!". Ruth Adelia Iverson had reference number 43613.
